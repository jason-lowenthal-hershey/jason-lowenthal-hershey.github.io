The Space Between Solutions: On Constraints, Collaboration, and Fixing One Thing
OUTLINE - Ready to Write

1. The Opening

Goal: Hook the reader with a personal observation about transitions or fixing something that seems simple but gets complicated.

Angle: Start with the idea of "invisible problems" - when fixing one thing creates another issue unexpectedly.

  • Personal reflection on how one small fix can cascade
  • The moment you realized "wait, now we have a different problem"
  • Lead into: this week was about that exact experience
Target: 150-250 words

2. What We Were Working On

Initial Context

Setting: The R2L (Ready-to-Launch) completion screen - that transition moment when users move to the next phase.

  • What is this screen's job? (communicate what's happening next, guide users forward)
  • Why does it matter? (it's a critical moment in the user journey)
  • The starting point: works great on desktop, broken on mobile

The Initial Fix

What happened: You made a clean fix that adjusted spacing and repositioned elements.

  • The fix felt right - button visible, text breathing properly
  • Looked good on your screen
  • Pushed to staging feeling satisfied

What Liza Found

The discovery: Your QA partner's video revealed the cascading problem.

  • The progress indicator moved above the content
  • Now it was cutting off text users needed to read
  • You'd solved one problem by creating another

Key Moment to Capture:

  • Your honest reaction: "GAH! How did I miss that?"
  • Liza's kind response about you juggling a lot
  • The humanity of that exchange - good teams hold space for missing things
Target: 200-300 words

3. How We Thought Through It

The Three Competing Needs

The core problem: Three essential things fighting for limited space.

  • Button visibility: Primary CTA can't be hidden/scrolled off
  • Content readability: Text needs to be legible and not cramped
  • Fixed viewport: Can't change the screen space itself

The real insight: you can't have it all, so what matters most?

The Collaborative Process

How you worked through it:

  • Reached out to Shantanu (design) rather than deciding alone
  • Explored possibilities together: scrolling within card? Reorganize? Make button scrollable?
  • Talked through tradeoffs rather than just picking one
  • Decided: make just the screenshot scrollable, keep button and copy fixed

Collaboration Moments to Highlight:

  • The back-and-forth with Shantanu about what matters
  • How you both prioritized button visibility as non-negotiable
  • The willingness to explore multiple solutions
  • Testing the approach, getting feedback, iterating

The Bigger Thinking

What guided your approach:

  • Not seeing constraint as problem, but as information
  • Asking "what do we really need here?" instead of "how do we fit it all?"
  • Treating the mobile viewport not as an obstacle, but as a teacher
Target: 200-300 words

4. What This Taught Us

About Constraints

Key insight: Constraints aren't failures of planning - they're information.

  • The mobile viewport forced clarity you might not have found otherwise
  • Limited space made you ask "what's essential?"
  • The constraint revealed what truly matters in the user experience

About Teamwork

What made the difference:

  • Liza's thoroughness (the video, the detail)
  • Her kindness in acknowledging you're juggling a lot
  • Shantanu engaging with it as a design challenge, not a dev problem
  • Everyone thinking about what was actually right, not what was easiest
Quote to consider: "You have a ton happening at once right now, friend." - This captures something important about how good teams hold space for each other's limits.

About Problem-Solving

The pattern you see:

  • Problems don't have one "right" answer - they have tradeoffs
  • The best solution balances competing needs thoughtfully
  • Collaboration isn't just nice - it catches what individuals miss
  • Iteration isn't failure - it's how you discover the right thing
Target: 200-250 words

5. Why This Matters

Connect to bigger picture: This wasn't just about one screen. It reveals how your team approaches all problems.

  • To people considering working with Codefi: This shows how thoughtfully you approach trade-offs
  • To builders/teams everywhere: Constraints are gifts, not obstacles
  • To the industry: Building great products requires both individual skill AND collaborative thinking

The broader insight: What you're really showing is that excellence comes from:

  • Seeing constraint as information, not failure
  • Trusting your team enough to ask for help
  • Caring more about what's right than what's easy
  • Holding space for each other's humanity while doing precise work
Target: 200-250 words

6. Looking Back

Bring it back around: Return to your opening about invisible problems and cascading fixes.

  • This week showed you that "something going wrong" can be the moment something goes right
  • The cascade of issues wasn't a failure - it was how you discovered what mattered
  • Working through problems together is what builds trust and capability

Final reflection:

  • What you're excited about (the team, the approach, what's coming next)
  • Why this week felt significant
  • What you hope readers take from this

End with something memorable about how you work together or what you're building.

Target: 150-200 words

Writing Tips Specific to This Week

Voice reminders:
  • Personal and warm - you're reflecting, not lecturing
  • Show your thinking process - readers want to see HOW you approach problems
  • Celebrate your team - Liza, Shantanu, and the whole team made this work
  • Be curious about what the week taught you
Moments to specifically capture:
  • Liza's video showing the problem (moment of discovery)
  • Your honest "GAH! How did I miss that?" reaction (humanness)
  • Liza's kind response (team culture)
  • Conversation with Shantanu about possibilities (collaboration)
  • The decision to prioritize button visibility (values)
Avoid:
  • Specific tech stack or tools (use "data store" not "PostgreSQL")
  • Internal jargon without explanation
  • Too much technical detail about the implementation
  • Making it sound like you had it all figured out (you didn't - that's the point!)
